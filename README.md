# Axis— Adjustable Desktop Phone Stand

Axis is a mechanically adjustable desktop phone stand designed in Autodesk Fusion 360. The goal was to create something a phone holder but incorporate multiple connected components, pivot points, and a side-mounted adjustment knob. I wanted the final design to be both practical and visually appealing, with an open structure and mechanical details that make the stand feel like a complete product.

## Features

- **Adjustable phone cradle:** Two raised support plates hold the phone in place, with lower retaining lips to prevent it from sliding forward.
- **Charging access:** A gap between the two support plates leaves room for a charging cable, particularly when the phone is positioned vertically.
- **Pivot-based adjustment:** Cylindrical connection points link the cradle to the rear supporting structure, allowing the upper section to be repositioned.
- **Textured adjustment knob:** A side-mounted cylindrical knob features repeated grip details, giving it a more mechanical appearance.
- **Four-point base:** An open, triangular-style base with four extended feet provides a stable foundation while avoiding an unnecessarily bulky design.
- **Separate mechanical components:** The stand is made up of individually modelled parts, including connecting pins, shafts, and supporting sections, however for this repo, the components are modeled together as how they would be in real life.

## Design Process

The design process began with researching existing adjustable phone stands. I wanted to understand how different stands supported a phone and how their mechanisms worked. This helped me settle on a pivot-based design, where the phone cradle connects to a rear support structure instead of just resting at a fixed angle.

Before modelling the full assembly, I broke Axis down into its main components. I also spent time practising screw and fastener modelling in Fusion 360, experimenting with the Thread and Coil features to better understand how threaded geometry and cylindrical connections could be used in a mechanical design.

### 1. Base Design

I started modelling the base because it would determine the proportions and positioning of the rest of the stand. Using sketches, lines, arcs, and extrusions, I created an open,  structure with four extended feet. I added holes at the corners for the feet and circular connection points for the supporting components.

My first version looked too bulky, so I went back and adjusted the sketch dimensions and extrusion thickness. I also removed sections from the middle to create a lighter, more open appearance. Some of the initial fillets were too large for the geometry, so I reduced their radius and applied them in smaller groups.

### 2. Phone Cradle

Next, I modelled the upper section that holds the phone. Instead of using one solid platform, I created two separate raised support plates. This gave the cradle a cleaner appearance while leaving space for a charging cable.

I added small retaining lips along the lower edges to stop the phone from sliding forward. I had to adjust their height and thickness because my first version stuck out too far. I also refined the spacing between the two plates using sketch dimensions and constraints to keep them symmetrical.

### 3. Rear Support Structure

The rear support connects the phone cradle to the base and provides the structure needed for angle adjustment. I created the main supporting sections using sketches and extrusions, then added cylindrical connection points for the pivot joints.

Getting these connections aligned was one of the more challenging parts of the design. Some of the initial holes and shafts did not line up properly, so I returned to the sketches and used reference geometry to position them more accurately. I also paid attention to the thickness around the connection points to keep the structure looking sturdy.

### 4. Adjustment Knob and Smaller Components

One of the main visual details of Axis is its side-mounted adjustment knob. I started with a simple cylindrical body and added a central connection section. The first version looked too plain, so I created repeated details around its outer surface to give it a textured grip which also made it easier to turn.

After completing the larger components, I modelled the smaller connecting pieces, including cylindrical pins and shafts. I used the techniques I had practised with threaded geometry to explore how fasteners could connect the different sections. I also considered the spacing and clearances between parts so that the assembly would fit together properly.

### 5. Final Assembly

Once all the individual components were complete, I brought them together in Fusion 360. I positioned the cradle above the rear support, aligned the pivot connections, attached the supporting structure to the base, and added the adjustment knob and smaller mechanical details.

During the first assembly, I noticed that some components overlapped slightly and that a few cylindrical sections were too close to surrounding geometry. I went back and adjusted their positions and clearances until everything was properly aligned.

After checking the model from different angles and refining the proportions, I completed the final Axis assembly.
